\section{The Hilbert-Poincaré series and the focused arc algebra}
\label{sec:hilb-poinc-seri}

All rings considered are commutative with identity element $1$, and all ring homomorphisms preserve the identity.
Let $A$ be a ring.
An \defterm{$A$-algebra} is a ring $B$ equipped with a ring homomorphism $A \to B$.
An \defterm{$\mathbb{N}$-graded ring} is a ring $R$ together with a family $(R_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ of additive subgroups of $R$ such that $R = \bigoplus_{n \in \mathbb{N}} R_n$ and $R_mR_n \subseteq R_{m + n}$.
Let $k$ be a field.
An \defterm{$\mathbb{N}$-graded $k$-algebra} is a $k$-algebra $A = \bigoplus_{n \in \mathbb{N}}A_n$ such that $A$ is an $\mathbb{N}$-graded ring, and each additive subgroup $A_n$ is a subspace of $A$.
If $a \in A_n$, we say $a$ has \defterm{weight} $n$.
This automatically implies $1 \in A_0$ and $k \subseteq A_0$.

Let $A$ be an $\mathbb{N}$-graded $k$-algebra.
The \defterm{Hilbert-Poincaré series of $A$} is defined by
\begin{equation*}
  \operatorname{HP}_A(t) = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{N}}\dim_k(A_n)t^n.
\end{equation*}
If each $A_n$ is finite dimensional, then $\operatorname{HP}_A(t) \in \mathbb{N}[[t]]$.

A \defterm{differential $k$-algebra} is a $k$-algebra $A$ together with a $k$-linear derivation $\partial \in \operatorname{Der}_k(A)$.
An ideal $I \subseteq A$ is \defterm{differential} if $\partial(I) \subseteq I$.
An \defterm{$\mathbb{N}$-graded differential $k$-algebra} is a differential $k$-algebra $A$ that is also an $\mathbb{N}$-graded $k$-algebra satisfying $\partial(A_n) \subseteq A_{n + 1}$.

Let $k$ be a field and let $A$ be a $k$-algebra.
Let $JA$ be the arc algebra of $A$.
We have a natural monomorphism $A \hookrightarrow JA$, so we can consider $A$ as a subset of $JA$ with $k[A]_\partial = JA$, where the notation $k[S]_\partial$ indicates the differential $k$-subalgebra generated by a subset $S$.
We define
\begin{equation*}
  (JA)_n = \operatorname{span}_k\{(\partial^{n_1}a_1)\dots(\partial^{n_s}a_s) \mid s, n_1, \dots, n_s \in \mathbb{N}, a_1, \dots, a_s \in A, n_1 + \dots + n_s = n\}.
\end{equation*}
Then
\begin{equation*}
  JA = \bigoplus_{n \in \mathbb{N}}(JA)_n,
\end{equation*}
and $JA$ becomes an $\mathbb{N}$-graded differential $k$-algebra.
We note that $(JA)_0 = A$.

Let $\mathfrak{p} \in \operatorname{Spec}(A)$.
Then $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a local ring with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p}$, and the quotient $\kappa(\mathfrak{p}) = A_\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p}$ is the \defterm{residue field of $A$ at $\mathfrak{p}$}.
We have a natural homomorphism $A \to \kappa(\mathfrak{p})$ which makes $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$ into an $A$-algebra.
We can also view $JA$ as an $A$-algebra.
The \defterm{focused arc algebra of $A$ at $\mathfrak{p}$} is the $\mathbb{N}$-graded $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$-algebra
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:1}
  J^\mathfrak{p}A = JA \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{p})
\end{equation}
with operation given by
\begin{equation*}
  (a\otimes x)(b\otimes y) = (ab)\otimes(xy)
\end{equation*}
and with $\mathbb{N}$-grading given by
\begin{equation*}
  (J^\mathfrak{p}A)_n = (JA)_n \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{p}) = \{a \otimes x \mid a \in (JA)_n, x \in \kappa(\mathfrak{p})\}.
\end{equation*}
We note that $\dim_{\kappa(\mathfrak{p})}((J^\mathfrak{p}A)_0) = 1$ because $(J^\mathfrak{p}A)_0 = A \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{p}) = \kappa(\mathfrak{p})$, and $J^\mathfrak{p}A$ does not inherit a derivation from $JA$.

\section{Concrete examples of the focused arc algebra}
\label{sec:concr-exampl-focus}

We are interested in the case where
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:2}
  A = k[X_1, \dots, X_n]/(F_1, \dots, F_m),
\end{equation}
where $F_1, \dots, F_m \in k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:3}
  0 = F_r(0, \dots, 0), \qquad r = 1, \dots, m,
\end{equation}
and $\mathfrak{p} = (X_1 + (F_1, \dots, F_m), \dots, X_n + (F_1, \dots, F_m))$ is a maximal ideal.
In this case, the residue field is $\kappa(\mathfrak{p}) = k$, and the natural homomorphism $A \to k$ is just evaluation at $(0, \dots, 0)$.
Informally, we can think of $JA$ as being the following set of solutions
\begin{equation*}
  JA = \{(x_1(t), \dots, x_n(t)) \in k[[t]]^n \mid 0 = F_r(x_1(t), \dots, x_n(t)), r = 1, \dots, m\}
\end{equation*}
in the same way we can think of $A$ as
\begin{equation*}
  A = \{(x_1, \dots, x_n) \in k^n \mid 0 = F_r(x_1, \dots, x_n), r = 1, \dots, m\}.
\end{equation*}
Of course, this is just an intuition because the $k$-algebra $A$ may be nonreduced.
For example, if $A = k[X]/(X^2)$ (a case we are interested in), then with this definition, we would get $A = JA = \{0\}$, which we obviously don't want.
We give two ways of constructing $JA$ and then show an isomorphism between them.

Using formal arcs: We consider the polynomial $k$-algebra $k[X^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n]$ in countably many variables $X^{(i)}_j$ with derivation $\partial \in \operatorname{Der}_k(k[X^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n])$ given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:4}
  \partial X^{(i)}_j = (i + 1)X^{(i + 1)}_j, \qquad i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n,
\end{equation}
where we assume $\operatorname{char}(k) = 0$.

To motivate this definition, we define:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:5}
  X_j(t) = X_j^{(0)} + X_j^{(1)}t + \dots \in k[X^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n][[t]], \qquad j = 1, \dots, n.
\end{equation}
We have a natural derivation $\partial_t$.
We wish to have
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:6}
  \partial(F(X_1(t), \dots, X_n(t))) = \partial_t(F(X_1(t), \dots, X_n(t))), \qquad F \in k[X_1, \dots, X_n].
\end{equation}
Taking $F = X_j, j = 1, \dots, n$, we get \eqref{eq:4}.
With this definition, we get \eqref{eq:6} for $F \in k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$.
Identifying $X_j = X^{(0)}_j, j = 1, \dots, n$, we have $k[X_1, \dots, X_n]_\partial = k[X^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n]$.

Following our intuition, we write
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:7}
  F_r(X_1(t), \dots, X_n(t)) = F_r^{(0)} + F_r^{(1)}t + \dots, \qquad r = 1, \dots, m,
\end{equation}
where $F_r^{(l)} \in k[X^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n], l \in \mathbb{N}, r = 1, \dots, m$.
Then $(F^{(l)}_r \mid l \in \mathbb{N}, r = 1, \dots, m)$ is a differential ideal because $\partial(F_r(X_1(t), \dots, X_n(t))) = \partial_t(F_r(X_1(t), \dots, X_n(t))), r = 1, \dots, m$ implies
\begin{equation*}
  \partial(F_r^{(l)}) = (l + 1)F_r^{(l + 1)}, \qquad l \in \mathbb{N}, r = 1, \dots, m.
\end{equation*}
Thus
\begin{equation*}
  F_r^{(l)} = \frac{\partial^lF_r^{(0)}}{l!}, \qquad l \in \mathbb{N}, r = 1, \dots, m.
\end{equation*}
From \eqref{eq:7}, we see that $F_r^{(0)} = F_r, r = 1, \dots, m$.
We have proved that
\begin{equation*}
  (F^{(l)}_r \mid l \in \mathbb{N}, r = 1, \dots, m) = (F_1, \dots, F_m)_\partial.
\end{equation*}
We set
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:8}
  JA = \frac{k[X^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n]}{(F^{(l)}_r \mid l \in \mathbb{N}, r = 1, \dots, m)} = \frac{k[X^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n]}{(F_1, \dots, F_m)_\partial},
\end{equation}
and we have a natural inclusion $A \hookrightarrow JA$ of $k$-algebras satisfying our desired universal property.

Using derivations: We consider the polynomial $k$-algebra $k[Y^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n]$ in countably many variables $Y^{(i)}_j$ with derivation $\partial \in \operatorname{Der}_k(k[Y^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n])$ given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:10}
  \partial Y^{(i)}_j = Y^{(i + 1)}_j, \qquad i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n.
\end{equation}
Identifying $Y_j = Y^{(0)}_j, j = 1, \dots, n$, we have $k[Y_1, \dots, Y_n]_\partial = k[Y^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n]$.

Following our intuition, we construct the arc algebra $JA$ by imposing the relations $F_r = 0$.
We define the sequence of higher derivatives $F_r^{(l)}$ by successively applying the derivation $\partial$.
That is, we define $F_r^{(0)} = F_r(Y^{(0)}_1, \dots, Y^{(0)}_n)$ and set
\begin{equation*}
  F_r^{(l)} = \partial^l(F_r^{(0)}), \qquad l \in \mathbb{N}, r = 1, \dots, m.
\end{equation*}
By definition, the differential ideal generated by $(F_1, \dots, F_m)$ is
\begin{equation*}
  (F_1, \dots, F_m)_\partial = (F^{(l)}_r \mid l \in \mathbb{N}, r = 1, \dots, m).
\end{equation*}
We then set
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:11}
  JA = \frac{k[Y^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n]}{(F_1, \dots, F_m)_\partial},
\end{equation}
and we have a natural inclusion $A \hookrightarrow JA$ of $k$-algebras satisfying our desired universal property.
We note that we haven't assumed $\operatorname{char}(k) = 0$ in this construction of the arc algebra.
If $\operatorname{char}(k) = 0$, these two differential algebras are obviously isomorphic with isomorphism given by $Y_j^{(i)} \mapsto i!X^{(i)}_j, i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n$.
Since the derivation approach does not require $\operatorname{char}(k) = 0$, we prefer it.

In both ways, setting
\begin{equation*}
  f^{(l)}_r = F_r^{(l)}|_{X^{(0)}_j \mapsto 0, j = 1, \dots, n} \in k[X^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{Z}_+, j = 1, \dots, n], \qquad l \in \mathbb{Z}_+, r = 1, \dots, m,
\end{equation*}
we have
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:9}
  J^0A = J^\mathfrak{p}A = JA \otimes_A k = \frac{k[X^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{Z}_+, j = 1, \dots, n]}{(f^{(l)}_r \mid l \in \mathbb{Z}_+, r = 1, \dots, m)}.
\end{equation}

\section{The grading of the arc algebra}
\label{sec:grading-arc-algebra}

Now we wish to define the grading of an arc algebra $JA$ where, as in the previous section,
\begin{equation*}
  A = k[X_1, \dots, X_n]/(F_1, \dots, F_m),
\end{equation*}
where $F_1, \dots, F_m \in k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ satisfy
\begin{equation*}
  0 = F_r(0, \dots, 0), \qquad r = 1, \dots, m.
\end{equation*}

However, let's take a look at the situation for a general polynomial $P \in k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$.
There are two ways to say when $P$ is homogeneous of degree $d$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item We let $\lambda$ be a variable, and we consider the polynomial $k[\lambda]$-algebra $k[\lambda][X_1, \dots, X_n]$.
  Then $P$ is homogeneous of degree $d$ if and only if $P(\lambda X_1, \dots, \lambda X_n) = \lambda^dP(X_1, \dots, X_n)$.
  This looks to be the cleanest approach.
\item The polynomial $P$ is homogeneous of degree $d$ if and only if,
  for all field extensions $k \to K$ and all $\lambda \in K^\times$,
  $P(\lambda X_1, \dots, \lambda X_n) = \lambda^dP(X_1, \dots, X_n)$.
  It is necessary to consider at least an infinite field extension because $k$ might be finite, and $\lambda \in K^\times$ is enough.
\end{enumerate}

Now we wish to do something similar with the weight of elements of $JA$.
First, we do this for polynomials $F$ in $k[X^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n]$.
As before, there are two ways to say when $F$ has weight $d$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item We let $\lambda$ be a variable, and we consider the polynomial $k[\lambda]$-algebra $k[\lambda][X^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n]$.
  Then $F$ has weight $d$ if and only if $(F)_{X^{(i)}_j \mapsto \lambda^iX^{(i)}_j} = \lambda^dF$.
\item We see that $F$ has weight $d$ if and only if $(F)_{X^{(i)}_j \mapsto \lambda^iX^{(i)}_j} = \lambda^dF$ for all field extensions $k \to K$ and all $\lambda \in K^\times$.
\end{enumerate}
Since $F_1, \dots, F_m$ are all homogeneous of weight $0$, the generators $F_r^{(l)} = \partial^l F_r$ are homogeneous of weight $l$ for all $l \in \mathbb{N}$ and $r = 1, \dots, m$.
Thus, $(F_1, \dots, F_m)_\partial$ is a homogeneous ideal, so we can define a grading on $JA$.

\begin{example}
  \label{exa:1}
  For $A = k[X]$, we have
  \begin{equation*}
    \operatorname{HP}_{J^0A}(t) = \prod_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_+}\frac{1}{1 - t^i}.
  \end{equation*}
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  \label{exa:2}
  For $A = k[X]/(X^2)$ with $\operatorname{char}(k) = 0$, we have
  \begin{equation*}
    \operatorname{HP}_{J^0A}(t) = \prod_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_+, i \equiv 1, 4 \bmod 5}\frac{1}{1 - t^i}.
  \end{equation*}
\end{example}

Now we move onto abstract schemes.
Again, we consider $k$ a field, and let $X$ be a $k$-scheme of finite type.
There are two ways of doing this:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Given a $k$-algebra $R$ and $a \in R$, we have two $k$-algebra homomorphisms
  \begin{align*}
    \psi_R: R[[t]] &\to R[\lambda][[t]], \\
    \psi_R(t) &= \lambda t, \\
    ev_a: R[\lambda][[t]] &\to  R[[t]], \\
    ev_a(\sum_{n \in \mathbb{N}}p_n(\lambda)t^n) &= \sum_{n \in \mathbb{N}}p_n(a)t^n.
  \end{align*}
  We note that $\lambda t \in(t)R[\lambda][[t]]$, so $\psi_R$ is continuous in the $(t)$-adic topologies on the domain and codomain.

  Composing these two $k$-algebra homomorphisms and taking spectra gives a $k$-scheme morphism
  \begin{equation*}
    \operatorname{Spec}(ev_a \circ \psi_R): \operatorname{Spec}(R[[t]]) \to \operatorname{Spec}(R[[t]]),
  \end{equation*}
  and pre-composing yields a function
  \begin{equation*}
    - \circ \operatorname{Spec}(ev_a \circ \psi_R): \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(R[[t]]), X) \to \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(R[[t]]), X).
  \end{equation*}
  However, by the universal property of arc spaces for $k$-schemes,
  \begin{equation*}
    \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(R[[t]]), X) = \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(R), JX).
  \end{equation*}
  So we can consider
  \begin{equation*}
    - \circ \operatorname{Spec}(ev_a \circ \psi_R): \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(R), JX) \to \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(R), JX)
  \end{equation*}
  which is a natural transformation in $R$.
  We have
  \begin{align*}
    \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(R), \mathbb{A}^1_k \times_k JX) &= \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(R), \mathbb{A}^1_k) \times \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(R), JX) \\
    &= R \times \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(R), JX).
  \end{align*}
  This gives us a natural map
  \begin{align*}
    R \times \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(R), JX) &\to \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(R), JX), \\
    (a, \gamma) &\mapsto (\gamma\circ \operatorname{Spec}(ev_a \circ \psi_R)) (\cong \gamma(at)).
  \end{align*}
  By Yoneda's Lemma, this is the same as giving an action of the multiplicative monoid $\mathbb{A}^1_k$ on the $k$-scheme $JX$
  \begin{equation*}
    \mu: \mathbb{A}^1_k \times_k JX \to JX.
  \end{equation*}
  Taking the global sections functor, the action $\mu$ gives rise to a $k$-algebra homomorphism
  \begin{equation*}
    \Gamma(\mu): \Gamma(JX) \to \Gamma(\mathbb{A}^1_k \times_k JX) = \Gamma(JX) \otimes_k \Gamma(\mathbb{A}^1_k) = \Gamma(JX) \otimes_k k[\lambda] = \Gamma(JX)[\lambda].
  \end{equation*}
  where the first equality holds because $JX$ is qcqs and $\mathbb{A}^1_k$ is affine.
  Finally, a global regular function $f \in \Gamma(JX)$ has \defterm{weight $d$} if
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:14}
    \Gamma(\mu)(f) = \lambda^df.
  \end{equation}
  When $X = \operatorname{Spec}(A), A = k[X_1, \dots, X_n]/(F_1, \dots, F_m)$, we know that
  \begin{equation*}
    \Gamma(JX) = JA = \frac{k[X^{(i)}_j \mid i \in \mathbb{N}, j = 1, \dots, n]}{(F_1, \dots, F_m)_\partial}.
  \end{equation*}
  It remains to show that the morphism $\Gamma(\mu): JA \to JA[\lambda]$ is given by $\overline{X^{(i)}_{j}}\mapsto \lambda^i\overline{X^{(i)}_j}$ to prove that our abstract definition coincides with the concrete algebraic definition given before.

  To see this,
  recall that by the universal property of $JX = \operatorname{Spec}(JA)$,
  the identity morphism $\mathrm{id}_{JX} \in \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(JA), JX)$ corresponds to the canonical universal arc
  \begin{equation*}
    \gamma_{\mathrm{univ}}: \operatorname{Spec}(JA[[t]]) \to X = \operatorname{Spec}(A),
  \end{equation*}
  whose comorphism $\Gamma(\gamma_{\mathrm{univ}}): A \to JA[[t]]$ is given on generators by
  \begin{equation*}
    \Gamma(\gamma_{\mathrm{univ}})(\overline{X_j}) = \sum_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \overline{X^{(i)}_j} t^i \in JA[[t]].
  \end{equation*}
  Now consider the action morphism $\mu: \mathbb{A}^1_k \times_k JX \to JX$.
  Since $\mathbb{A}^1_k \times_k JX \cong \operatorname{Spec}(JA[\lambda])$,
  the morphism $\mu$ corresponds under the universal property of $JX$ to an arc
  \begin{equation*}
    \tilde{\mu}: \operatorname{Spec}(JA[\lambda][[t]]) \to X = \operatorname{Spec}(A).
  \end{equation*}
  By definition of the action $\mu$,
  the arc $\tilde{\mu}$ is obtained by scaling the parameter of $\gamma_{\mathrm{univ}}$ by $\lambda$,
  which is the pre-composition with $\operatorname{Spec}(\psi_{JA}): \operatorname{Spec}(JA[\lambda][[t]]) \to \operatorname{Spec}(JA[[t]])$ where $\psi_{JA}(t) = \lambda t$.
  On coordinate rings,
  the comorphism $\Gamma(\tilde{\mu}): A \to JA[\lambda][[t]]$ is therefore the composite $\psi_{JA} \circ \Gamma(\gamma_{\mathrm{univ}})$:
  \begin{align*}
    \Gamma(\tilde{\mu})(\overline{X_j}) &= \psi_{JA}\left(\sum_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \overline{X^{(i)}_j} t^i\right) \\
    &= \sum_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \overline{X^{(i)}_j} (\lambda t)^i \\
    &= \sum_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \left(\lambda^i \overline{X^{(i)}_j}\right) t^i \in JA[\lambda][[t]].
  \end{align*}
  On the other hand, by the universal property of the arc algebra $JA$, the comorphism on global sections $\Gamma(\mu): JA \to JA[\lambda]$ satisfies
  \begin{equation*}
    \Gamma(\tilde{\mu})(\overline{X_j}) = \sum_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \Gamma(\mu)\left(\overline{X^{(i)}_j}\right) t^i.
  \end{equation*}
  Equating the coefficients of $t^i$ in $JA[\lambda][[t]]$,
  we obtain
  \begin{equation*}
    \Gamma(\mu)\left(\overline{X^{(i)}_j}\right) = \lambda^i \overline{X^{(i)}_j},
  \end{equation*}
  as desired.
\item For any field extension $k \to K$ and any scalar $\lambda \in K^\times$, we have an automorphism of $K$-algebras
\begin{align*}
  \varphi_\lambda:K[[t]] &\to K[[t]], \\
  t &\mapsto \lambda t.
\end{align*}
This automorphism induces a natural scheme morphism
\begin{equation*}
  \operatorname{Spec}(\varphi_\lambda): \operatorname{Spec}(K[[t]]) \to \operatorname{Spec}(K[[t]]),
\end{equation*}
which yields a natural bijection
\begin{equation*}
  - \circ \operatorname{Spec}(\varphi_\lambda): \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(K[[t]]), X) \to \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(K[[t]]), X).
\end{equation*}
Since $\operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(K[[t]]), X) = \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(K), JX) = JX(K)$, for $\lambda \in K^\times$, we have a bijection
\begin{equation*}
  \Phi_\lambda: JX(K) \to JX(K),
\end{equation*}
which simply corresponds to mapping a formal arc $x(t) \in JX(K)$ to $x(\lambda t)$.
Because $JX(K)$ is merely a set, we cannot directly apply algebraic functors to it.
To recover the rigorous algebraic automorphism defined earlier, we must lift this geometric action back into the category of schemes.
We do this in four steps.

\subsubsection*{1. Base change}
Because we want to evaluate everything over the field extension $K$, we mathematically lift the underlying scheme $X$ to $K$ via the fiber product $X_K = X \times_k \operatorname{Spec}(K)$.

\subsubsection*{2. Arc schemes commute with base change}
A fundamental and powerful property of arc schemes is that their formation commutes with base change.
This means that the arc scheme of the base-changed scheme $X_K$ is naturally isomorphic to the base change of the original arc scheme: $J_K(X_K) \cong (JX)_K$.
Because of this isomorphism, the set of points $JX(K)$ can naturally be viewed as the $K$-valued points of the $K$-scheme $J_K(X_K)$.

\subsubsection*{3. Inducing the scheme automorphism}
Since $J_K(X_K)$ represents a functor of points on all $K$-algebras, its $S$-valued points for any $K$-algebra $S$ are $\operatorname{Hom}_K(\operatorname{Spec}(S[[t]]), X_K)$.
The automorphism $\varphi_\lambda: K[[t]] \to K[[t]]$ extends canonically to an $S$-algebra automorphism mapping $t \mapsto \lambda t$.
This induces a natural transformation on the functor of points via pre-composition $\gamma \mapsto \gamma \circ \operatorname{Spec}(\varphi_{\lambda, S})$.
By Yoneda's Lemma, this natural transformation corresponds to a unique $K$-scheme automorphism
\begin{equation*}
  \tilde{\Phi}_\lambda: (JX)_K \to (JX)_K
\end{equation*}
on the base-changed scheme itself.

\subsubsection*{4. Applying the global sections functor}
The global sections operator $\Gamma$ is a contravariant functor from the category of schemes to the category of commutative rings.
Applying $\Gamma$ to the $K$-scheme automorphism $\tilde{\Phi}_\lambda$ yields a ring automorphism $\Gamma(\tilde{\Phi}_\lambda): \Gamma((JX)_K) \to \Gamma((JX)_K)$ on its ring of global sections.
Since $JX$ is qcqs and $\operatorname{Spec}(K)$ is affine over $k$,
we have $\Gamma((JX)_K) = \Gamma(JX) \otimes_k K$,
so we recover the algebraic automorphism
\begin{equation*}
  \Gamma(\tilde{\Phi}_\lambda): \Gamma(JX) \otimes_k K \to \Gamma(JX) \otimes_k K.
\end{equation*}
We define an element $f \in \Gamma(JX)$ to be algebraically homogeneous of weight $d$ if
\begin{equation*}
  \Gamma(\tilde{\Phi}_\lambda)(f\otimes1) = f\otimes\lambda^d
\end{equation*}
for all field extensions $k \to K$ and all $\lambda \in K^\times$.
By defining this homogeneity entirely over field extensions,
we bypass the need for arbitrary test rings while still protecting against finite-field collapses.
This definition is equivalent to the one given in approach~(1):
the $\mathbb{A}^1_k$-action $\mu$ specializes,
for each field extension $k \to K$ and each $\lambda \in K^\times$,
to the automorphism $\tilde{\Phi}_\lambda$ via the evaluation homomorphism $k[\lambda] \to K$.
\end{enumerate}
We prefer the first approach because it is more economical.

We define the \defterm{weight-$d$ subspace} of $\Gamma(JX)$ to be
\begin{equation*}
  \Gamma(JX)_d = \{f \in \Gamma(JX) \mid \Gamma(\mu)(f) = \lambda^d f\}.
\end{equation*}

\begin{proposition}
  \label{prop:weight-decomposition}
  Let $X$ be a $k$-scheme of finite type.
  Then
  \begin{equation*}
    \Gamma(JX) = \bigoplus_{d \in \mathbb{N}} \Gamma(JX)_d.
  \end{equation*}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Since $JX$ is qcqs and $\mathbb{A}^1_k$ is affine over $k$,
  the coaction on global sections lands in the polynomial ring:
  \begin{equation*}
    \sigma := \Gamma(\mu): \Gamma(JX) \to \Gamma(JX) \otimes_k k[\lambda] = \Gamma(JX)[\lambda].
  \end{equation*}
  For any $f \in \Gamma(JX)$,
  write $\sigma(f) = \sum_{d = 0}^{N} f_d \lambda^d$,
  which is a finite sum because $\sigma$ lands in the polynomial ring $\Gamma(JX)[\lambda]$.

  \textbf{Unit axiom.}
  The unit $e: \operatorname{Spec}(k) \to \mathbb{A}^1_k$ has comorphism $k[\lambda] \to k$, $\lambda \mapsto 1$.
  The monoid action axiom $\mu \circ (e \times \mathrm{id}_{JX}) = \mathrm{id}_{JX}$
  gives on global sections:
  \begin{equation*}
    f = \sigma(f)\big|_{\lambda = 1} = \sum_{d = 0}^{N} f_d.
  \end{equation*}

  \textbf{Associativity axiom.}
  The multiplication $m: \mathbb{A}^1_k \times_k \mathbb{A}^1_k \to \mathbb{A}^1_k$
  has comorphism $k[\lambda] \to k[\lambda_1, \lambda_2]$, $\lambda \mapsto \lambda_1\lambda_2$.
  The axiom $\mu \circ (\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{A}^1_k} \times \mu) = \mu \circ (m \times \mathrm{id}_{JX})$
  gives on global sections:
  \begin{equation*}
    \sum_{d = 0}^{N} \sigma(f_d)\,\lambda_2^d
    = \sum_{d = 0}^{N} f_d\,(\lambda_1\lambda_2)^d
    = \sum_{d = 0}^{N} f_d\,\lambda_1^d\,\lambda_2^d,
  \end{equation*}
  where the left side is obtained by applying $\sigma$ to each coefficient $f_d$
  (using $\lambda_1$ as the indeterminate),
  and the right side is obtained by substituting $\lambda \mapsto \lambda_1\lambda_2$ in $\sigma(f)$.
  Equating coefficients of $\lambda_2^d$,
  we obtain $\sigma(f_d) = \lambda_1^d f_d$,
  i.e., each $f_d$ lies in $\Gamma(JX)_d$.

  \textbf{Uniqueness.}
  The components $f_d$ are the coefficients of $\lambda^d$ in $\sigma(f) \in \Gamma(JX)[\lambda]$,
  hence are uniquely determined by $f$.
  Since $f = \sum_d f_d$ and each $f_d \in \Gamma(JX)_d$,
  the decomposition $\Gamma(JX) = \bigoplus_{d \in \mathbb{N}} \Gamma(JX)_d$ follows.
\end{proof}

Now let $X$ be a $k$-scheme of finite type,
and let $\mathfrak{p} \in X$ be a point with residue field $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$.
For $m \in \mathbb{N}$, the truncation morphisms $A[[t]] \to A[t]/(t^{m + 1})$ for $k$-algebras $A$ give rise to a canonical morphism $\psi_m: JX \to X_m$ to the $m$-th jet scheme $X_m$.
In particular, since $X_0 = X$, we have a canonical morphism $\psi_0: JX \to X$.
We also have a canonical morphism of schemes $i_\mathfrak{p}: \operatorname{Spec}(\kappa(\mathfrak{p})) \to X$.
Thus, we can form the fiber product
\begin{equation*}
  J^\mathfrak{p}X = JX \times_X \operatorname{Spec}(\kappa(\mathfrak{p})),
\end{equation*}
which is called the \defterm{focused arc scheme of $X$ at $\mathfrak{p}$} and is an affine scheme over $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$.
The \defterm{focused arc algebra of $X$ at $\mathfrak{p}$} is the ring of global sections $\Gamma(J^\mathfrak{p}X)$, which is a $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$-algebra.

If $U = \operatorname{Spec}(A) \subseteq X$ is an affine open neighborhood of $\mathfrak{p}$,
so that $\mathfrak{p}$ corresponds to a prime ideal $\mathfrak{p} \subseteq A$,
then
\begin{equation*}
  J^\mathfrak{p}X \cong \operatorname{Spec}(JA) \times_{\operatorname{Spec}(A)} \operatorname{Spec}(\kappa(\mathfrak{p}))
  \cong \operatorname{Spec}(JA \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{p})).
\end{equation*}
Taking global sections,
we obtain a canonical isomorphism of $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$-algebras
\begin{equation*}
  \Gamma(J^\mathfrak{p}X) \cong JA \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{p}) = J^\mathfrak{p}A,
\end{equation*}
showing that this definition is independent of the choice of affine open neighborhood
and coincides with the affine definition given in \eqref{eq:1}.

To understand the induced action on the focused arc scheme $J^\mathfrak{p}X$ in detail,
recall that $J^\mathfrak{p}X$ is defined by the Cartesian square
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fiber-pullback}
  \begin{tikzcd}
    J^\mathfrak{p}X \arrow[r, "j"] \arrow[d, "p"'] & JX \arrow[d, "\psi_0"] \\
    \operatorname{Spec}(\kappa(\mathfrak{p})) \arrow[r, "i_\mathfrak{p}"'] & X
  \end{tikzcd}
\end{equation}
where $j: J^\mathfrak{p}X \to JX$ is the canonical morphism from the fiber,
and $p: J^\mathfrak{p}X \to \operatorname{Spec}(\kappa(\mathfrak{p}))$ is the structure morphism.

First,
the scaling action $\mu: \mathbb{A}^1_k \times_k JX \to JX$ preserves the projection $\psi_0$.
Functorially,
for any $k$-algebra $R$,
an element of $(\mathbb{A}^1_k \times_k JX)(R)$ is a pair $(\lambda, \gamma)$
with $\lambda \in R$ and $\gamma \in \operatorname{Hom}_k(\operatorname{Spec}(R[[t]]), X)$.
The action $\mu$ maps $(\lambda, \gamma)$ to the scaled arc $\gamma_\lambda(t) := \gamma(\lambda t)$,
and the projection $\psi_0$ evaluates an arc at $t = 0$.
Since $\gamma_\lambda(0) = \gamma(0)$,
evaluating at $t = 0$ is independent of the scaling factor $\lambda$.
Hence,
the following diagram commutes:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mu-pi0-commute}
  \begin{tikzcd}
    \mathbb{A}^1_k \times_k JX \arrow[r, "\mu"] \arrow[d, "\mathrm{pr}_2"'] & JX \arrow[d, "\psi_0"] \\
    JX \arrow[r, "\psi_0"'] & X
  \end{tikzcd}
\end{equation}
that is,
$\psi_0 \circ \mu = \psi_0 \circ \mathrm{pr}_2$.

Next,
base-changing $\mathbb{A}^1_k$ along $\operatorname{Spec}(\kappa(\mathfrak{p})) \to \operatorname{Spec}(k)$ gives
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{A}^1_{\kappa(\mathfrak{p})} \times_{\kappa(\mathfrak{p})} J^\mathfrak{p}X
  \cong \mathbb{A}^1_k \times_k J^\mathfrak{p}X.
\end{equation*}
Consider the composite morphism $\Phi := \mu \circ (\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{A}^1} \times j): \mathbb{A}^1_k \times_k J^\mathfrak{p}X \to JX$.
Using the commutativity of \eqref{eq:mu-pi0-commute} and \eqref{eq:fiber-pullback},
we have
\begin{equation*}
  \psi_0 \circ \Phi = \psi_0 \circ \mathrm{pr}_2 \circ (\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{A}^1} \times j)
  = \psi_0 \circ j \circ \mathrm{pr}_2
  = i_\mathfrak{p} \circ (p \circ \mathrm{pr}_2),
\end{equation*}
where $\mathrm{pr}_2: \mathbb{A}^1_k \times_k J^\mathfrak{p}X \to J^\mathfrak{p}X$ is the projection onto the second factor.
By the universal property of the fiber product $J^\mathfrak{p}X$,
there exists a unique morphism
\begin{equation*}
  \mu_\mathfrak{p}: \mathbb{A}^1_{\kappa(\mathfrak{p})} \times_{\kappa(\mathfrak{p})} J^\mathfrak{p}X \to J^\mathfrak{p}X
\end{equation*}
making the following diagram commute:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mu-p-universal-diagram}
  \begin{tikzcd}
    \mathbb{A}^1_{\kappa(\mathfrak{p})} \times_{\kappa(\mathfrak{p})} J^\mathfrak{p}X \arrow[dd, "p \circ \mathrm{pr}_2"'] \arrow[dr, dashed, "\exists! \, \mu_\mathfrak{p}"] \arrow[rr, "\Phi = \mu \circ (\mathrm{id} \times j)"] & & JX \arrow[dd, "\psi_0"] \\
    & J^\mathfrak{p}X \arrow[ur, "j"'] \arrow[dl, "p"] & \\
    \operatorname{Spec}(\kappa(\mathfrak{p})) \arrow[rr, "i_\mathfrak{p}"'] & & X
  \end{tikzcd}
\end{equation}
This morphism $\mu_\mathfrak{p}$ defines the scaling action of the multiplicative monoid $\mathbb{A}^1_{\kappa(\mathfrak{p})}$ on $J^\mathfrak{p}X$.

Taking global sections,
$\mu_\mathfrak{p}$ induces a coaction
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma_\mathfrak{p}: \Gamma(J^\mathfrak{p}X) \to \Gamma(J^\mathfrak{p}X)[\lambda],
\end{equation*}
which on an affine open neighborhood $U = \operatorname{Spec}(A) \subseteq X$ of $\mathfrak{p}$
recovers the algebraic coaction $\sigma \otimes \mathrm{id}_{\kappa(\mathfrak{p})}: JA \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{p}) \to (JA \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{p}))[\lambda]$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:weight-decomposition},
this induces a weight decomposition
\begin{equation*}
  \Gamma(J^\mathfrak{p}X) = \bigoplus_{d \in \mathbb{N}} \Gamma(J^\mathfrak{p}X)_d,
\end{equation*}
making $\Gamma(J^\mathfrak{p}X)$ an $\mathbb{N}$-graded $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$-algebra
with $\Gamma(J^\mathfrak{p}X)_0 = \kappa(\mathfrak{p})$.
The \defterm{arc Hilbert-Poincaré series of $X$ at $\mathfrak{p}$} is then defined by
\begin{equation*}
  \operatorname{HP}_{\Gamma(J^\mathfrak{p}X)}(t) = \sum_{d \in \mathbb{N}} \dim_{\kappa(\mathfrak{p})}(\Gamma(J^\mathfrak{p}X)_d) t^d \in \mathbb{N}[[t]].
\end{equation*}

\section{The Focused Arc Algebra of the Non-Reduced Node and Rogers--Ramanujan Identities}
\label{sec:focused-arc-algebra-xy2}

Let $k$ be a field of characteristic zero, let $n \in \mathbb{Z}_+$, and let $s \in \{1, \dots, n\}$.
For $m \in \mathbb{N}$, let $(t)_m = \prod_{i = 1}^m (1 - t^i)$ denote the $q$-Pochhammer symbol (with $(t)_0 = 1$).
The Andrews--Gordon generalization of the Rogers--Ramanujan identities states that
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{k = (k_1, \dots, k_{n - 1}) \in \mathbb{N}^{n - 1}}\frac{t^{\frac{1}{2}kG^{(n)}k^\top + kB^{(n)}_{n - s}}}{(t)_{k_1}\dots(t)_{k_{n - 1}}} = \prod^\infty_{\substack{i = 1 \\ i \not\equiv 0, \pm s \bmod 2n + 1}}(1 - t^i)^{-1},
\end{equation*}
where
\begin{align*}
  G^{(n)} &= (2\min\{i, j\})_{i, j = 1}^{n - 1}, \\
  B^{(n)}_j &= (\underbrace{0, \dots, 0}_{n - 1 - j}, 1, 2, \dots, j)^\top \in \mathbb{N}^{n - 1} \quad \text{for $j = 0, 1, \dots, n - 1$},
\end{align*}
and ${}^\top$ denotes the transpose matrix (in particular, $B^{(n)}_0 = (0, \dots, 0)^\top$).
We denote by $AG_{n, s}(t) \in \mathbb{N}[[t]]$ either side of this identity (though it's probably better to think of the products when doing Hilbert-Poincaré series).

Recall that
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{H}(t) = \prod_{i=1}^\infty \frac{1}{1 - t^i} = \sum_{m=0}^\infty p(m)t^m \in \mathbb{N}[[t]]
\end{equation}
is the generating function for integer partitions.

\begin{conjecture}
\label{conj:xy2-hilbert-series}
For $A = k[X, Y] / ((XY)^2)$, we have
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:conj-xy2-hp}
  \operatorname{HP}_{J^0A}(t) = \frac{\mathbb{H}(t)}{1 - t}AG_{2, 1}(t).
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
in Euler product form,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:conj-xy2-euler}
  \operatorname{HP}_{J^0A}(t) = \prod_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{(1 - t^k)^{c_k}},
\end{equation}
where the exponents $c_k$ satisfy $c_1 = 2$ and,
for all $k \ge 2$,
\begin{equation}
  c_k = \begin{cases}
    2 & \text{if } k \equiv \pm 2 \pmod 5, \\
    1 & \text{if } k \equiv 0, \pm 1 \pmod 5.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
\end{conjecture}

\begin{conjecture}[Generalization to $(XY)^n$]
\label{conj:xyn-generalization}
Let $n \ge 2$ be an integer.
For the algebra $A_n = k[X, Y]/((XY)^n)$, we have
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{HP}_{J^0A_n}(t) = \frac{\mathbb{H}(t)}{1 - t}AG_{n, 1}(t).
\end{equation}
\end{conjecture}

\subsection{The Base Change Framework for General $f$}
\label{subsec:base-change-general}

We now provide a conceptual proof of Conjecture~\ref{conj:xy2-hilbert-series} and Conjecture~\ref{conj:xyn-generalization}
which avoids any computation of $S$-polynomials or Gröbner bases in two variables.
More generally,
we consider an arbitrary polynomial $f \in k[X_1, \dots, X_r]$ vanishing at the origin,
with order of vanishing $d = \operatorname{ord}_0(f) \ge 1$.

Let $P = k[X_j^{(i)} \mid 1 \le j \le r, \, i \ge 1]$ be the coordinate ring of the focused arc space of $\mathbb{A}^r$ at the origin,
endowed with the standard grading $\operatorname{wt}(X_j^{(i)}) = i$.
A formal arc centered at the origin is given by
\begin{equation}
  X_j(t) = \sum_{i = 1}^\infty X_j^{(i)} t^i \in P[[t]].
\end{equation}
Substituting this arc into $f$ yields
\begin{equation}
  f(X_1(t), \dots, X_r(t)) = \sum_{l = d}^\infty u_l \, t^l = t^d \sum_{j = 0}^\infty u_{j + d} \, t^j \in P[[t]],
\end{equation}
where each $u_l \in P$ is homogeneous of weight $l$,
and $u_0 = u_1 = \dots = u_{d - 1} \equiv 0$.

Now let $Q = k[W^{(d)}, W^{(d + 1)}, \dots]$ be an infinite polynomial ring with grading $\operatorname{wt}(W^{(l)}) = l$.
We define a homomorphism of $\mathbb{N}$-graded $k$-algebras
\begin{equation}
  \phi_f \colon Q \longrightarrow P, \qquad W^{(l)} \longmapsto u_l = [t^l] f(X_1(t), \dots, X_r(t)),
\end{equation}
where $[t^l]$ denotes the coefficient of $t^l$.
Geometrically,
$\phi_f$ is the comorphism of the morphism of focused arc spaces induced by $f \colon \mathbb{A}^r \to \mathbb{A}^1$.

Now consider the non-reduced hypersurface $Y = \{f^n = 0\} \subset \mathbb{A}^r$.
Its defining ideal in $P$ is generated by the coefficients of $(f(X_1(t), \dots, X_r(t)))^n$,
that is,
\begin{equation}
  I_\infty^0(f^n) = \bigl( [t^l] (f(X_1(t), \dots, X_r(t)))^n \bigm| l \ge n d \bigr) \subset P.
\end{equation}
In the base ring $Q$,
let $W(t) = \sum_{l = d}^\infty W^{(l)} t^l$.
The ideal
\begin{equation}
  J_n = \bigl( [t^l] W(t)^n \bigm| l \ge n d \bigr) \subset Q
\end{equation}
defines the 1-variable fat point arc algebra $B_n := Q / J_n$.
Notice that
\begin{equation}
  I_\infty^0(f^n) = \phi_f(J_n) \cdot P.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Base Change Isomorphism]
\label{prop:base-change-iso}
For any polynomial $f \in k[X_1, \dots, X_r]$ with $f(0) = 0$ and any integer $n \ge 1$,
there is a canonical isomorphism of $\mathbb{N}$-graded $k$-algebras:
\begin{equation}
  J^0\bigl(k[X_1, \dots, X_r] / (f^n)\bigr) \cong P \otimes_Q B_n,
\end{equation}
where $B_n = Q / J_n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the right-exactness of the tensor product,
for any ring homomorphism $\phi \colon Q \to P$ and any ideal $J \subset Q$,
we have $P \otimes_Q (Q / J) \cong P / (\phi(J) P)$.
Since $I_\infty^0(f^n) = \phi_f(J_n) P$,
the claim follows immediately.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Role of Flatness and $\operatorname{Tor}$ Modules}
\label{subsec:flatness-tor}

Proposition~\ref{prop:base-change-iso} expresses the non-reduced focused arc algebra as a tensor product.
To deduce the Hilbert--Poincar\'{e} series,
we recall the homological relationship between tensor products and Hilbert series.

\begin{proposition}[Euler--Poincar\'{e} Formula for Graded Tensor Products]
\label{prop:euler-poincare-tor}
Let $Q$ be an $\mathbb{N}$-graded $k$-algebra with $Q_0 = k$ and finite-dimensional graded components,
and let $P$ and $B$ be $\mathbb{N}$-graded $Q$-modules with finite-dimensional graded components.
Assume that $B$ admits a minimal (or degree-wise finite) graded free resolution $F_\bullet \to B \to 0$ over $Q$.
Then
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:tor-expansion-notes}
  \sum_{i = 0}^\infty (-1)^i \operatorname{HP}_{\operatorname{Tor}_i^Q(P, B)}(t) = \frac{\operatorname{HP}_P(t) \cdot \operatorname{HP}_B(t)}{\operatorname{HP}_Q(t)}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{HP}_{P \otimes_Q B}(t) = \frac{\operatorname{HP}_P(t) \cdot \operatorname{HP}_B(t)}{\operatorname{HP}_Q(t)} + \sum_{i = 1}^\infty (-1)^{i - 1} \operatorname{HP}_{\operatorname{Tor}_i^Q(P, B)}(t).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $\dots \to F_2 \to F_1 \to F_0 \to B \to 0$ be a minimal graded free resolution of $B$ over $Q$,
so that in each weight $m$ only finitely many $(F_i)_m$ are non-zero.
Tensoring with $P$ yields the complex $P \otimes_Q F_\bullet$,
whose homology modules are by definition $\operatorname{Tor}_i^Q(P, B)$.
By the additivity of the Hilbert--Poincar\'{e} series on exact sequences of graded vector spaces,
the alternating sum of the Hilbert--Poincar\'{e} series of the homology modules equals the alternating sum of the chains:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i = 0}^\infty (-1)^i \operatorname{HP}_{\operatorname{Tor}_i^Q(P, B)}(t) = \sum_{i = 0}^\infty (-1)^i \operatorname{HP}_{P \otimes_Q F_i}(t).
\end{equation}
Since each $F_i$ is a free $Q$-module,
$\operatorname{HP}_{P \otimes_Q F_i}(t) = \frac{\operatorname{HP}_P(t)}{\operatorname{HP}_Q(t)} \operatorname{HP}_{F_i}(t)$.
Applying additivity again to $F_\bullet \to B \to 0$ yields $\sum (-1)^i \operatorname{HP}_{F_i}(t) = \operatorname{HP}_B(t)$,
which completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Flatness and Factored Hilbert Series]
\label{cor:flatness-criterion}
Suppose $P$ is a \textbf{flat} $Q$-module.
Then $\operatorname{Tor}_i^Q(P, B) = 0$ for all $i \ge 1$,
and the Hilbert--Poincar\'{e} series factors cleanly:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{HP}_{P \otimes_Q B}(t) = \frac{\operatorname{HP}_P(t)}{\operatorname{HP}_Q(t)} \cdot \operatorname{HP}_B(t).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

Because $Q = k[W^{(d)}, W^{(d + 1)}, \dots]$ is a polynomial ring over a field,
graded flatness has a concrete characterization:
$P$ is flat over $Q$ if and only if the sequence of elements $(u_d, u_{d + 1}, \dots)$ forms a \textbf{regular sequence} in $P$ in every finite truncation.
Notice that the quotient $P / (u_d, u_{d + 1}, \dots)$ is precisely the focused arc algebra of the \textbf{reduced} hypersurface $f = 0$:
\begin{equation}
  P / (u_d, u_{d + 1}, \dots) \cong J^0\bigl(k[X_1, \dots, X_r] / (f)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\textbf{$P$ is flat over $Q$ if and only if the focused jet schemes of the reduced hypersurface $f = 0$ are complete intersections!}
Whenever this holds,
the prefactor is given by the reduced arc Hilbert--Poincar\'{e} series:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\operatorname{HP}_P(t)}{\operatorname{HP}_Q(t)} = \operatorname{HP}_{J^0(f = 0)}(t).
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Shifted 1-Variable Fiber $B_n$}
\label{subsec:shifted-fiber}

We now determine $\operatorname{HP}_{B_n}(t)$ for the fiber algebra $B_n = Q / J_n$.
Recall that $Q = k[W^{(d)}, W^{(d + 1)}, \dots]$ with $\operatorname{wt}(W^{(l)}) = l$.
Define $V^{(j)} := W^{(j + d - 1)}$ for $j \ge 1$.
Then
\begin{equation}
  W(t) = \sum_{l = d}^\infty W^{(l)} t^l = t^{d - 1} \sum_{j = 1}^\infty V^{(j)} t^j =: t^{d - 1} V(t).
\end{equation}
Then $W(t)^n = t^{n(d - 1)} V(t)^n$,
so the relations $[t^l] W(t)^n = 0$ are identical to $[t^{l - n(d - 1)}] V(t)^n = 0$.
Thus,
as an ungraded ring,
$B_n$ is isomorphic to the focused arc algebra of the 1-variable fat point $V^n = 0$.
However,
the weights of the variables are shifted by $d - 1$:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{wt}(V^{(j)}) = \operatorname{wt}(W^{(j + d - 1)}) = j + (d - 1).
\end{equation}

By the theorem of Bruschek, Mourtada, and Schepers~\cite{bruschek_arc_2013},
the focused arc algebra of $V^n = 0$ has a basis of standard monomials indexed by partitions $\lambda = (\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_m \ge 1)$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_i - \lambda_{i + n - 1} \ge 2 \quad \text{for all } i.
\end{equation}
Under the weight shift,
a monomial of length $m$ has weight
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{wt}(V^{(\lambda_1)} \dots V^{(\lambda_m)}) = \sum_{i = 1}^m (\lambda_i + d - 1) = |\lambda| + m(d - 1).
\end{equation}
Setting $\mu_i := \lambda_i + d - 1$,
the transformed partition $\mu$ satisfies the same gap condition $\mu_i - \mu_{i + n - 1} \ge 2$,
but its smallest part satisfies $\mu_m = \lambda_m + d - 1 \ge d$.
Therefore:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fiber-general-formula-notes}
  \operatorname{HP}_{B_n}(t) = \sum_{\substack{\mu \\ \mu_i - \mu_{i + n - 1} \ge 2 \\ \mu_{\min} \ge d}} t^{|\mu|}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Proof of the Conjectures for $(XY)^n = 0$}
\label{subsec:proof-conjectures}

We now specialize to $A_n = k[X, Y] / ((XY)^n)$.
Here $r = 2$,
and $f(X, Y) = XY$,
with vanishing order $d = 2$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:regularity-node-notes}
The sequence $(u_2, u_3, \dots)$ is a regular sequence in $P = k[X^{(i)}, Y^{(i)} \mid i \ge 1]$.
Consequently,
$P$ is flat over $Q = k[W^{(2)}, W^{(3)}, \dots]$,
and all higher $\operatorname{Tor}_i^Q(P, B_n)$ vanish for $i \ge 1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In any finite truncation $N \ge 2$,
consider the $N - 1$ polynomials $u_2, \dots, u_N$ in the polynomial ring $P_{\le N} = k[X^{(1)}, \dots, X^{(N)}, Y^{(1)}, \dots, Y^{(N)}]$ of dimension $2N$.
The zero locus $V(u_2, \dots, u_N) \subset \mathbb{A}^{2N}$ consists of arcs $(X(t), Y(t))$ with $X(t)Y(t) \equiv 0 \pmod{t^{N + 1}}$.
Let $\operatorname{ord}(X) = a \ge 1$ and $\operatorname{ord}(Y) = b \ge 1$.
Then $a + b \ge N + 1$.
The irreducible components (corresponding to minimal prime ideals) occur when $a + b = N + 1$,
where $X^{(1)} = \dots = X^{(a - 1)} = 0$ and $Y^{(1)} = \dots = Y^{(b - 1)} = 0$.
The codimension of each such component is
\begin{equation}
  (a - 1) + (b - 1) = a + b - 2 = N - 1.
\end{equation}
Since $N - 1$ equations define an algebraic set of pure codimension $N - 1$ in the Cohen--Macaulay ring $P_{\le N}$,
the sequence $(u_2, \dots, u_N)$ is a regular sequence for all $N$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Hilbert--Poincar\'{e} Series of $(XY)^n$]
\label{thm:proof-xyn-notes}
For all $n \ge 2$,
the Hilbert--Poincar\'{e} series of the focused arc algebra of $A_n = k[X, Y] / ((XY)^n)$ is
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{HP}_{J^0 A_n}(t) = \frac{\mathbb{H}(t)}{1 - t} AG_{n, 1}(t),
\end{equation}
establishing Conjectures~\ref{conj:xy2-hilbert-series} and~\ref{conj:xyn-generalization}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:base-change-iso},
$J^0 A_n \cong P \otimes_Q B_n$.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:regularity-node-notes},
$P$ is flat over $Q$,
so Corollary~\ref{cor:flatness-criterion} implies
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{HP}_{J^0 A_n}(t) = \frac{\operatorname{HP}_P(t)}{\operatorname{HP}_Q(t)} \cdot \operatorname{HP}_{B_n}(t).
\end{equation}
For the prefactor,
we have
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\operatorname{HP}_P(t)}{\operatorname{HP}_Q(t)} = \frac{\prod_{i = 1}^\infty \frac{1}{(1 - t^i)^2}}{\prod_{k = 2}^\infty \frac{1}{1 - t^k}}
  = \frac{\mathbb{H}(t)^2}{(1 - t)\mathbb{H}(t)} = \frac{\mathbb{H}(t)}{1 - t}.
\end{equation}
For the fiber,
since $d = 2$,
equation~\eqref{eq:fiber-general-formula-notes} shows that $\operatorname{HP}_{B_n}(t)$ counts partitions with difference $\mu_i - \mu_{i + n - 1} \ge 2$ and $\mu_{\min} \ge 2$ (no parts of size $1$).
By Gordon's combinatorial generalization of the Rogers--Ramanujan identities~\cite{gordon_combinatorial_1961} specialized to $s = 1$,
this generating function is precisely $AG_{n, 1}(t)$.
This completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Generalization to Normal Crossings $(X_1 \cdots X_r)^n = 0$}
\label{subsec:normal-crossings}

The same argument generalizes directly to higher-dimensional normal crossings singularities.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:normal-crossings-notes}
Let $r \ge 2$,
and let $A_{r, n} = k[X_1, \dots, X_r] / ((X_1 \cdots X_r)^n)$.
Then:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The sequence $u_l = [t^l](X_1(t) \cdots X_r(t))$ for $l \ge r$ is a regular sequence in $P = k[X_j^{(i)} \mid 1 \le j \le r, \, i \ge 1]$.
  \item The reduced prefactor is:
  \begin{equation}
    \frac{\operatorname{HP}_P(t)}{\operatorname{HP}_Q(t)} = \frac{\mathbb{H}(t)^{r - 1}}{(1 - t)(1 - t^2)\cdots(1 - t^{r - 1})} = \frac{\mathbb{H}(t)^{r - 1}}{(t)_{r - 1}}.
  \end{equation}
  \item The Hilbert--Poincar\'{e} series is given by:
  \begin{equation}
    \operatorname{HP}_{J^0 A_{r, n}}(t) = \frac{\mathbb{H}(t)^{r - 1}}{(t)_{r - 1}} \sum_{\substack{\mu \\ \mu_i - \mu_{i + n - 1} \ge 2 \\ \mu_{\min} \ge r}} t^{|\mu|}.
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
In particular,
for $n = 2$,
we obtain the shifted Rogers--Ramanujan series:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{HP}_{J^0 A_{r, 2}}(t) = \frac{\mathbb{H}(t)^{r - 1}}{(t)_{r - 1}} \sum_{m = 0}^\infty \frac{t^{m^2 + (r - 1)m}}{(t)_m}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Here $d = r$.
In each truncation $N \ge r$,
the condition $\prod X_j(t) \equiv 0 \pmod{t^{N + 1}}$ with $\operatorname{ord}(X_j) = a_j \ge 1$ requires $\sum a_j \ge N + 1$.
The irreducible components (corresponding to minimal prime ideals) satisfy $\sum a_j = N + 1$,
imposing $\sum (a_j - 1) = N + 1 - r$ vanishing coordinates $X_j^{(1)} = \dots = X_j^{(a_j - 1)} = 0$ in $\mathbb{A}^{r N}$.
The number of equations is $N - r + 1$.
Because codimension equals the number of equations,
the jet scheme is a complete intersection,
proving regularity.
The weight shift on the BMS basis is $d - 1 = r - 1$,
enforcing $\mu_{\min} \ge r$.
When $n = 2$,
any partition $\mu = (\mu_1, \dots, \mu_m)$ with $\mu_i - \mu_{i + 1} \ge 2$ and $\mu_m \ge r$ has minimal weight $\sum_{i = 1}^m (r + 2(m - i)) = m^2 + (r - 1)m$,
yielding the $q$-series sum over $m \ge 0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Homological Obstruction for Non-Flat Singularities}
\label{subsec:obstruction-nonflat}

It is instructive to observe where this factorization fails.
Consider a non-squarefree monomial such as $f(X, Y) = X^2 Y$.
The order is $d = 3$.
In truncation $N \ge 4$,
we have $N - 2$ equations $u_3, \dots, u_N$.
An arc with $\alpha = \operatorname{ord}(X) \ge 1$ and $\beta = \operatorname{ord}(Y) \ge 1$ satisfies $X(t)^2 Y(t) \equiv 0 \pmod{t^{N + 1}}$ if $2\alpha + \beta \ge N + 1$.
Taking $\alpha = 2$ and $\beta = N - 3 \ge 1$ gives an irreducible component where $X^{(1)} = 0$ and $Y^{(1)} = \dots = Y^{(N - 4)} = 0$.
The codimension of this component is
\begin{equation}
  (2 - 1) + (N - 4) = N - 3 < N - 2.
\end{equation}
Because the codimension is strictly smaller than the number of equations,
the jet scheme is \textbf{not} a complete intersection!
Consequently:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The sequence $(u_3, u_4, \dots)$ is \textbf{not} regular in $P$.
  \item $P$ is \textbf{not} flat over $Q$.
  \item The module $\operatorname{Tor}_1^Q(P, B_n)$ does not vanish:
    specifically, $u_3 = (X^{(1)})^2 Y^{(1)}$ and $u_4 = X^{(1)}(2X^{(2)}Y^{(1)} + X^{(1)}Y^{(2)})$ satisfy the relation $(2X^{(2)}Y^{(1)} + X^{(1)}Y^{(2)})u_3 = X^{(1)}Y^{(1)}u_4$ in $P$,
    which induces a non-trivial syzygy of weight $3n + 3$ between the first two generators $\phi_f((W^{(3)})^n)$ and $\phi_f(n(W^{(3)})^{n - 1}W^{(4)})$ of $\phi_f(J_n)$,
    whereas the minimal syzygy in $Q$ has weight $3n + 4$.
\end{itemize}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:euler-poincare-tor},
the true Hilbert--Poincar\'{e} series receives correction terms from $\operatorname{Tor}_1^Q(P, B_n)$,
preventing a simple product decomposition.
This explains why squarefree base functions $f = X_1 \cdots X_r$ are precisely the family for which clean Rogers--Ramanujan and Andrews--Gordon factorizations hold.
